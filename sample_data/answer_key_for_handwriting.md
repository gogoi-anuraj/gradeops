## Q1 — Newton's First Law (4 marks total)

**Variant A — Full marks (4/4)**
> Newton's First Law states that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues moving at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a net external force. This is also called the law of inertia.
>
> An inertial frame of reference is a frame of reference in which Newton's First Law holds true — that is, a frame that is either at rest or moving at constant velocity (not accelerating). In such a frame, an object with no net force acting on it will not appear to accelerate.
>
> Example: A ball placed on a table in a car moving at constant velocity on a straight road will stay still relative to the car, since the car is (approximately) an inertial frame. But if the car suddenly brakes, the ball will appear to roll forward — this is because the car's frame is no longer inertial (it's accelerating).

**Variant B — Partial marks (2/4)** — misses inertial frame explanation
> Newton's First Law says an object will stay at rest or keep moving at the same speed unless a force acts on it. This is called inertia.
>
> Example: If you slide a book across a table, it eventually stops because of friction acting on it as an external force. Without friction, it would keep sliding forever.
>
> *(Note: does not explain inertial reference frame at all — should lose the 1 mark for that criterion, and the example given is more about friction than about illustrating the law directly, so partial credit only)*

**Variant C — Mostly wrong (1/4)**
> Newton's First Law is about force equals mass times acceleration. If there is more mass, you need more force to move it.
>
> Example: pushing a heavy box is harder than pushing a light box.
>
> *(This is actually describing the Second Law, not the First — should score very low, maybe 1 mark for at least mentioning force/motion in a loosely related way)*

## Q2 — Block on frictionless surface, F = 20 N, m = 5 kg, find acceleration (6 marks total)

**Variant A — Full marks (6/6)**
> Free-body diagram: Weight (w) acting downward, Normal force (N) acting upward, Applied force (F = 20 N) acting horizontally to the right.
>
> Since the surface is frictionless and vertical forces balance (N = w), the only unbalanced force is the applied horizontal force.
>
> Using Newton's Second Law: F_net = m × a
> 20 = 5 × a
> a = 20 / 5
> **a = 4 m/s²** (in the direction of the applied force)

**Variant B — Partial marks (4/6)** — correct setup, arithmetic slip
> Free-body diagram: weight down, normal force up, applied force F = 20N horizontal.
>
> F = ma
> 20 = 5a
> a = 10 m/s²
>
> *(Diagram and equation setup correct — full marks there — but final arithmetic is wrong: 20/5 = 4, not 10. Loses the final-answer marks.)*

**Variant C — Mostly wrong (2/6)**
> The block has weight and the force pushing it. Since there's a force of 20N, the acceleration must also be related to gravity somehow.
>
> a = F/m = 20/5 = 4 m/s² but then multiply by g = 9.8
> a = 39.2 m/s²
>
> *(Correctly identifies F=ma and even gets 4 m/s² momentarily, but then incorrectly brings in gravity — which is irrelevant here since motion is horizontal on a frictionless surface. No proper free-body diagram shown. Partial credit for the equation setup attempt only.)*

## Q3 — Newton's Third Law, swimmer pushing off wall (4 marks total)

**Variant A — Full marks (4/4)**
> Newton's Third Law states that for every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force. The two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.
>
> When a swimmer pushes off a wall, the swimmer exerts a force on the wall (action), and the wall exerts an equal and opposite force back on the swimmer (reaction). This reaction force is what propels the swimmer forward into the water.
>
> These two forces do not cancel each other out because they act on two different objects — the action force acts on the wall, while the reaction force acts on the swimmer. Forces can only cancel if they act on the same object.

**Variant B — Partial marks (2/4)** — misses "different objects" explanation
> Newton's third law says every action has an equal and opposite reaction.
>
> When the swimmer pushes the wall, the wall pushes back on the swimmer, and that's what makes the swimmer move forward.
>
> *(Correct statement and correct example, but doesn't explain why the forces don't cancel — missing the "acts on different objects" reasoning, so loses that final mark.)*

**Variant C — Mostly wrong (1/4)**
> Newton's third law is when forces balance out so nothing moves, like the swimmer staying still against the wall before pushing off.
>
> *(Misunderstands the law entirely — confuses it with force balance/equilibrium rather than action-reaction pairs. No correct explanation of the swimmer example as an action-reaction pair.)*

## Q4 — Box pulled with friction, m = 10 kg, T = 40 N, μ = 0.2, g = 9.8 m/s² (8 marks total)

**Variant A — Full marks (8/8)**
> Free-body diagram: Tension (T = 40N) acting horizontally forward, friction force (f) acting horizontally backward (opposing motion), weight (w = mg) acting downward, normal force (N) acting upward.
>
> Since there's no vertical acceleration: N = mg = 10 × 9.8 = 98 N
>
> Friction force: f = μN = 0.2 × 98 = 19.6 N
>
> Net force equation (horizontal): F_net = T − f = 40 − 19.6 = 20.4 N
>
> Using Newton's Second Law: F_net = ma
> 20.4 = 10 × a
> **a = 2.04 m/s²**

**Variant B — Partial marks (5/8)** — forgets friction depends on N, uses wrong N
> Free-body diagram: tension forward, friction backward, weight down, normal force up.
>
> Friction: f = μ × m = 0.2 × 10 = 2 N
>
> F_net = T − f = 40 − 2 = 38 N
>
> a = F_net / m = 38 / 10 = 3.8 m/s²
>
> *(Diagram correct, net force equation setup correct, but friction formula is wrong — used f = μm instead of f = μN = μmg. This is a common mistake, so it's a good test case for whether the grader catches a conceptual error mid-derivation rather than just checking the final number.)*

**Variant C — Mostly wrong (2/8)**
> The box has tension pulling it and friction slowing it down.
>
> a = T/m = 40/10 = 4 m/s²
>
> *(No free-body diagram, ignores friction entirely in the calculation despite mentioning it exists, so the answer is essentially just T/m with no correct treatment of friction. Very low partial credit — maybe for identifying the relevant forces in words.)*
