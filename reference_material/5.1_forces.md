# 5.1 Forces

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, you will be able to:

- Identify simple free-body diagrams

- Describe force as a vector

## Kinematics vs. Dynamics

The study of motion is called kinematics, but kinematics only describes the way objects move — their velocity and their acceleration. Dynamics is the study of how forces affect the motion of objects and systems. It considers the causes of motion of objects and systems of interest, where a system is anything being analyzed. The foundation of dynamics are the laws of motion stated by Isaac Newton (1642–1727). These laws provide an example of the breadth and simplicity of principles under which nature functions. They are also universal laws in that they apply to situations on Earth and in space.

Newton's laws of motion produce a good description of motion only when objects are moving at speeds much less than the speed of light and are larger than the size of most molecules (about 10⁻⁹ m in diameter). These constraints define the realm of Newtonian mechanics. At the beginning of the twentieth century, Albert Einstein (1879–1955) developed the theory of relativity, and quantum mechanics was developed alongside it, neither of which share the constraints of Newtonian physics. All situations considered in this chapter are within the realm of Newtonian physics.

*Figure 5.2: Isaac Newton (1642–1727) published Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica in 1687, proposing the laws of motion that still apply today. Newton also discovered the law of gravity, invented calculus, and contributed to the theories of light and color.*

## Working Definition of Force

Dynamics is the study of the forces that cause objects and systems to move. An intuitive definition of force — a push or a pull — is a good starting point. A push or a pull has both magnitude and direction, making it a vector quantity. Force can be represented by vectors or expressed as a multiple of a standard force.

The push or pull on an object can vary considerably in magnitude or direction. For example, a cannon exerts a strong force on a cannonball launched into the air, while Earth exerts only a tiny downward pull on a flea. Since force is a vector, it adds like other vectors — using the head-to-tail method or trigonometric methods.

*Figure 5.3: (a) An overhead view of two ice skaters pushing on a third skater with forces F1 and F2, perpendicular to each other. The resultant vector F_total forms the hypotenuse of the triangle formed by placing F1 and F2 head to tail. (b) A free-body diagram representing the forces acting on the third skater, with F1 and F2 originating from the same point.*

Figure 5.3(b) is the first example of a **free-body diagram**: a sketch showing all external forces acting on an object or system. The object or system is represented by a single isolated point (the free body), and only external forces — those originating outside the object or system — are shown. Internal forces within the body are ignored, since only external forces acting on the free body affect its motion. Forces are represented by vectors extending outward from the free body.

### Problem-Solving Strategy: Drawing Free-Body Diagrams
1. Draw the object under consideration. If treating the object as a particle, represent it as a point at the origin of an xy-coordinate system.
2. Include all forces that act on the object, representing them as vectors. Do not include the net force on the object or forces the object exerts on its environment.
3. Resolve all force vectors into x- and y-components.
4. Draw a separate free-body diagram for each object in the problem.

*Figure 5.4: Two examples of free-body diagrams. (a) A box at rest on a horizontal surface, with the normal force vector pointing upward and the weight vector pointing downward. (b) A box on an inclined plane, with the weight vector pointing straight down, the normal force perpendicular to the plane, and a friction force vector pointing up along the plane. N is the normal force, w is the weight of the object, and f is the friction.*

## Development of the Force Concept

A quantitative definition of force can be based on a standard force, just as distance is measured relative to a standard length. One possibility: stretch a spring a fixed distance and use the restoring force it exerts to pull itself back to its relaxed shape as a standard. The magnitude of all other forces can then be considered as multiples of this standard unit of force.

*Figure 5.5: The force exerted by a stretched spring used as a standard unit of force. (a) A spring at undistorted length x. (b) When stretched a distance Δx, the spring exerts a reproducible restoring force F_restore. (c) A spring scale uses a spring to measure force; here the force has a magnitude of six units of the force standard.*

Consider a physics student sitting at a table, working on his homework. What external forces act on him?

*Figure 5.6: (a) The forces acting on the student are due to the chair, the table, the floor, and Earth's gravitational attraction — force C (chair, upward) and W (weight, downward) act along the line of his torso; force T (table, upward) acts near his forearms; force F (floor, upward) acts near his feet. (b) A free-body diagram considering only the forces C and W acting along the line running through his torso.*

In most situations, forces are grouped into two categories:

- **Contact forces**: due to direct physical contact between objects. In the example above, these are forces C, F, and T — exerted by the chair on the student's posterior, the floor on his feet, and the table on his forearms.
- **Field forces**: act without physical contact between objects, depending on the presence of a "field" in the surrounding region of space. Since the student is in Earth's gravitational field, he feels a gravitational force w — he has weight.

There are only four fundamental force fields in nature: gravitational, electromagnetic, strong nuclear, and weak. The gravitational field is responsible for weight. Electromagnetic field forces include static electricity and magnetism, and are responsible for the attraction among atoms in bulk matter. The strong nuclear and weak force fields are effective only over distances roughly equal to the scale of an atomic nucleus (10⁻¹⁵ m), so neither has influence in the macroscopic world of Newtonian mechanics.

Contact forces are fundamentally electromagnetic in origin — e.g., when an elbow contacts a tabletop, atomic charges in the skin interact electromagnetically with charges in the table's surface, producing the net contact force. However, in the context of Newtonian mechanics, this electromagnetic origin is not an important concern.

## Vector Notation for Force

Force is a vector — it has both magnitude and direction. The SI unit of force is the **newton** (N), where:

**1 N = 1 kg·m/s²**

(the force needed to accelerate a 1 kg object at a rate of 1 m/s²). A useful way to remember the size of a newton: a small apple weighs about 1 N.

A two-dimensional force can be written as **F = a·î + b·ĵ**, and a three-dimensional force as **F = Fx·î + Fy·ĵ + Fz·k̂**, where î, ĵ, k̂ are unit vectors along the x, y, and z axes.

**Worked Example — Adding forces (Figure 5.3 scenario):**
If skater 1 pushes horizontally with 30.0 N to the right: **F1 = 30.0î N**
If skater 2 pushes with 40.0 N in the positive vertical direction: **F2 = 40.0ĵ N**

The net external force is the vector sum of all external forces:

**F_net = ΣF = F1 + F2 + ...**  (Eq. 5.1)

For this example: **F_net = F1 + F2 = 30.0î + 40.0ĵ N**

Magnitude of the resultant (using the Pythagorean rule):

**F_net = √[(30.0 N)² + (40.0 N)²] = 50.0 N**

Direction, measured from the positive x-axis:

**θ = tan⁻¹(F2 / F1) = tan⁻¹(40.0 / 30.0) = 53.1°**

**Second example:** If **F1 = 3.0î + 8.0ĵ N** and **F2 = 5.0î + 4.0ĵ N**, adding component-wise:

**F_net = F1 + F2 = (3.0î + 8.0ĵ) + (5.0î + 4.0ĵ) = 8.0î + 12ĵ N**
