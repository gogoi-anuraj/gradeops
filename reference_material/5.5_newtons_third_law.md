We have thus far considered force as a push or a pull; however, if you think about it, you realize that no push or pull ever occurs by itself. When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you. This brings us to Newton’s third law.

---

## Newton’s Third Law of Motion

> **Newton’s Third Law of Motion**  
> Whenever one body exerts a force on a second body, the first body experiences a force that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force that it exerts. Mathematically, if body A exerts a force $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{AB}}$ on body B, then body B simultaneously exerts a force $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{BA}}$ on body A, expressed in vector form as:
>
> $$\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{AB}} = -\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{BA}} \tag{5.10}$$

Newton’s third law represents a certain symmetry in nature: Forces always occur in pairs, and one body cannot exert a force on another without experiencing a force itself. We sometimes refer to this law loosely as “action-reaction,” where the force exerted is the action and the force experienced as a consequence is the reaction. Newton’s third law has practical uses in analyzing the origin of forces and understanding which forces are external to a system.

We can readily see Newton’s third law at work by taking a look at how people move about. Consider a swimmer pushing off the side of a pool (**Figure 5.16**). She pushes against the wall of the pool with her feet and accelerates in the direction opposite that of her push. The wall has exerted an equal and opposite force on the swimmer. You might think that two equal and opposite forces would cancel, but they do not because they act on different systems. 

![Figure 5.16](figure_5_16_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 5.16 When the swimmer exerts a force on the wall, she accelerates in the opposite direction; in other words, the net external force on her is in the direction opposite of $F_{\text{feet on wall}}$. The free-body diagram shows only $F_{\text{wall on feet}}$, $\vec{\mathbf{w}}$ (gravitational force), and $BF$ (buoyant force).*

In this case, there are two systems that we could investigate: the swimmer and the wall. If we select the swimmer to be the system of interest, then $F_{\text{wall on feet}}$ is an external force on this system and affects its motion. The swimmer moves in the direction of this force. In contrast, $F_{\text{feet on wall}}$ acts on the wall, not on our system of interest. Thus, $F_{\text{feet on wall}}$ does not directly affect the motion of the system and does not cancel $F_{\text{wall on feet}}$. In a free-body diagram, we never include both forces of an action-reaction pair.

### Examples of Newton’s Third Law

- **Walking/Pacing**: As a professor paces in front of a whiteboard, he exerts a force backward on the floor. The floor exerts a reaction force forward on the professor that causes him to accelerate forward.
- **Car Acceleration**: A car accelerates forward because the ground pushes forward on the drive wheels, in reaction to the drive wheels pushing backward on the ground.
- **Rocket Propulsion**: Rockets move forward by expelling gas backward at high velocity. The rocket exerts a large backward force on the gas in its combustion chamber; therefore, the gas exerts a large reaction force forward on the rocket (thrust). Rockets work better in a vacuum where they can more readily expel exhaust gases.
- **Helicopters, Birds, and Airplanes**: Helicopters create lift by pushing air down, thereby experiencing an upward reaction force. Birds and airplanes force air downward and backward to get lift and move forward.
- **Marine Propulsion**: An octopus propels itself in the water by ejecting water through a funnel from its body.
- **Ropes & Climbing**: When a person pulls down on a vertical rope, the rope pulls up on the person (**Figure 5.17**).

![Figure 5.17](figure_5_17_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 5.17 When the mountain climber pulls down on the rope, the rope pulls up on the mountain climber.*

There are two important features of Newton’s third law:
1. **Equal and Opposite**: The forces exerted (action and reaction) are always equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.
2. **Different Bodies**: These forces act on different bodies or systems ($A$'s force acts on $B$, and $B$'s force acts on $A$). Thus, they do not cancel each other.

### Example 5.9: Forces on a Stationary Object

The package in **Figure 5.19** is sitting on a scale. For the scale and package at rest (i.e., not accelerating), show that the scale indicates the weight of the package.

![Figure 5.19](figure_5_19_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 5.19 (a) Forces on a package sitting on a scale along with their reaction forces. (b) Isolation of the package-scale system and the package-Earth system.*

#### Strategy
The forces on the package are $\vec{\mathbf{S}}$ (due to the scale) and $-\vec{\mathbf{w}}$ (due to Earth's gravitational field). The reaction forces that the package exerts are $-\vec{\mathbf{S}}$ on the scale and $\vec{\mathbf{w}}$ on Earth. Because the package is not accelerating, we apply Newton's second law.

#### Solution
$$\vec{\mathbf{S}} - \vec{\mathbf{w}} = m\vec{\mathbf{a}} = \vec{\mathbf{0}}$$
$$\vec{\mathbf{S}} = \vec{\mathbf{w}}$$

The scale reading indicates the magnitude of the force $\vec{\mathbf{S}}$, which is equal to the magnitude of the package’s weight.

#### Significance
The scale does not measure the weight of the package directly; it measures the force $-\vec{\mathbf{S}}$ on its surface. If the system were accelerating, $\vec{\mathbf{S}}$ and $-\vec{\mathbf{w}}$ would not be equal.

### Example 5.10: Getting Up to Speed: Choosing the Correct System

A physics professor pushes a cart of demonstration equipment to a lecture hall (**Figure 5.20**). Her mass is $65.0\text{ kg}$, the cart’s mass is $12.0\text{ kg}$, and the equipment’s mass is $7.0\text{ kg}$. Calculate the acceleration produced when the professor exerts a backward force of $150\text{ N}$ on the floor. All forces opposing the motion, such as friction on the cart’s wheels and air resistance, total $24.0\text{ N}$.

![Figure 5.20](figure_5_20_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 5.20 A professor pushes the cart with her demonstration equipment.*

#### Strategy
Define the system as the professor, cart, and equipment (**System 1**). The floor exerts a forward reaction force $F_{\text{floor}} = 150\text{ N}$ on System 1. Friction $f = 24.0\text{ N}$ opposes the motion. Internal forces ($F_{\text{prof}}$ or $F_{\text{cart}}$) are not included because they act within the system and cancel out.

#### Solution
Calculate net external force:

$$F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{floor}} - f = 150\text{ N} - 24.0\text{ N} = 126\text{ N}$$

Calculate total mass of System 1:

$$m = 65.0\text{ kg} + 12.0\text{ kg} + 7.0\text{ kg} = 84.0\text{ kg}$$

Apply Newton’s second law:

$$a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{126\text{ N}}{84.0\text{ kg}} = 1.5\text{ m/s}^2$$

#### Significance
Internal forces between components of a system cancel out because they are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. Choosing System 1 simplifies finding the acceleration of the entire group.

### Example 5.11: Force on the Cart: Choosing a New System

Calculate the force the professor exerts on the cart in **Figure 5.20**, using data from the previous example if needed.

#### Strategy
Define the system as the cart plus the equipment (**System 2**). The force the professor exerts on the cart, $F_{\text{prof}}$, is an external force acting on System 2.

#### Solution
Net external force on System 2 is:

$$F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{prof}} - f \implies F_{\text{prof}} = F_{\text{net}} + f$$

Calculate $F_{\text{net}}$ using mass of System 2 ($m = 12.0\text{ kg} + 7.0\text{ kg} = 19.0\text{ kg}$) and acceleration ($a = 1.5\text{ m/s}^2$):

$$F_{\text{net}} = ma = (19.0\text{ kg})(1.5\text{ m/s}^2) = 28.5\text{ N} \approx 29\text{ N}$$

Solve for $F_{\text{prof}}$:

$$F_{\text{prof}} = F_{\text{net}} + f = 28.5\text{ N} + 24.0\text{ N} = 52.5\text{ N} \approx 53\text{ N}$$

#### Significance
This force ($53\text{ N}$) is significantly less than the $150\text{-N}$ force exerted backward on the floor because some of the total force accelerates the professor herself.
