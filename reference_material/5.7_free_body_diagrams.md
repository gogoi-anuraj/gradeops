# 5.7 Drawing Free-Body Diagrams

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this section, you will be able to:
- Explain the rules for drawing a free-body diagram
- Construct free-body diagrams for different situations

---

The first step in describing and analyzing most phenomena in physics involves the careful drawing of a free-body diagram[cite: 11]. Remember that a free-body diagram must **only include the external forces acting on the body of interest**[cite: 11]. Once we have drawn an accurate free-body diagram, we can apply:
* **Newton’s First Law**: If the body is in equilibrium (balanced forces; $F_{\text{net}} = 0$)[cite: 11].
* **Newton’s Second Law**: If the body is accelerating (unbalanced force; $F_{\text{net}} \neq 0$)[cite: 11].

---

## Problem-Solving Strategy: Constructing Free-Body Diagrams

Observe the following rules when constructing a free-body diagram[cite: 11]:

1. **Isolate the Object**: Draw the object under consideration[cite: 11]. You may circle the object of interest to focus strictly on forces acting *on* it[cite: 11]. If treating the object as a particle, represent it as a point (often placed at the origin of an $xy$-coordinate system)[cite: 11].
2. **Identify External Forces**: Include all forces acting on the object as vectors (e.g., normal force, friction, tension, spring force, weight, applied force)[cite: 11].
   * **Do NOT** include the net force ($F_{\text{net}}$)[cite: 11].
   * **Do NOT** include forces exerted *by* the object on its environment[cite: 11].
   * **Do NOT** include both forces of an action-reaction pair[cite: 11].
3. **Resolve Components**: Convert vectors into $x$- and $y$-components when needed[cite: 11]. Place a squiggly line through the original vector to show it has been replaced by its components[cite: 11].
4. **Multiple Bodies**: Draw a **separate** free-body diagram for each object in the problem[cite: 11].

> **Note on Acceleration**: If the object accelerates, do **not** draw acceleration directly on the free-body diagram as a force vector[cite: 11]. You may indicate acceleration outside the diagram in a separate color[cite: 11].

### Sled Example Breakdown

When pulling a sled by force $\vec{\mathbf{P}}$ at an angle of $30^\circ$[cite: 11]:
- **Step 1 & 2**: Show external vectors $\vec{\mathbf{P}}$ (up and right), $\vec{\mathbf{N}}$ (up), and $\vec{\mathbf{w}}$ (down)[cite: 11].
- **Step 3**: Resolve $\vec{\mathbf{P}}$ into horizontal ($P_x = P \cos 30^\circ$) and vertical ($P_y = P \sin 30^\circ$) components[cite: 11].

### Example 5.14: Two Blocks on an Inclined Plane

Construct free-body diagrams for object A and object B resting on an incline[cite: 11]. Object A is pulled upward along the slope by a rope, while object B slides down the slope underneath object A[cite: 11].

#### Solution

**Object A (Top Block):**
- **Tension ($\vec{\mathbf{T}}$)**: Points up the incline (parallel to slope)[cite: 11].
- **Weight ($\vec{\mathbf{w}}_A$)**: Points vertically downward[cite: 11].
- **Normal Force ($\vec{\mathbf{N}}_{BA}$)**: Exerted by B on A, directed perpendicular and away from the interface[cite: 11].
- **Friction Force ($\vec{\mathbf{f}}_{BA}$)**: Exerted by B on A[cite: 11]. Because object A moves upward relative to B, friction acts **downward** parallel to the plane[cite: 11].

**Object B (Bottom Block):**
- **Weight ($\vec{\mathbf{w}}_B$)**: Points vertically downward[cite: 11].
- **Ramp Normal Force ($\vec{\mathbf{N}}_B$)**: Exerted by incline on B (perpendicular, pointing up/left)[cite: 11].
- **Ramp Friction ($\vec{\mathbf{f}}_B$)**: Exerted by incline on B, acting **upward** parallel to the ramp to oppose sliding down[cite: 11].
- **Contact Normal Force ($\vec{\mathbf{N}}_{AB}$)**: Exerted by A on B (perpendicular, pointing down/right)[cite: 11].
- **Contact Friction ($\vec{\mathbf{f}}_{AB}$)**: Exerted by A on B (Third Law pair to $\vec{\mathbf{f}}_{BA}$), acting **upward** parallel to the ramp[cite: 11].

#### Significance
Circling each object independently ensures internal interface forces (like action-reaction pairs $\vec{\mathbf{f}}_{BA}$ and $\vec{\mathbf{f}}_{AB}$) are directed correctly[cite: 11].

### Example 5.15: Two Blocks in Contact

A horizontal force $\vec{\mathbf{F}}$ pushes block $m_1$ to the right, which is in contact with block $m_2$[cite: 11]. Draw the interface reaction forces[cite: 11].

#### Solution

- **Block $m_1$**: Experiences applied force $\vec{\mathbf{F}}$ pointing right, weight $m_1\vec{\mathbf{g}}$ down, normal force from floor up, and reaction contact force $\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{21}$ pointing **left** (action of block 2 on block 1)[cite: 11].
- **Block $m_2$**: Experiences weight $m_2\vec{\mathbf{g}}$ down, normal force from floor up, and contact force $\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{12}$ pointing **right** (reaction of block 1 on block 2)[cite: 11].

#### Significance
$\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{21} = -\vec{\mathbf{A}}_{12}$ according to Newton's Third Law[cite: 11].

### Example 5.16: Block on the Table (Coupled Blocks)

A block $m_1$ rests on a table and is attached via a light rope over a pulley to a suspended block $m_2$[cite: 11].

#### Solution

- **Block $m_1$ (on table)**:
  - Vertical: Normal force $\vec{\mathbf{N}}$ (up), Weight $m_1\vec{\mathbf{g}}$ (down)[cite: 11].
  - Horizontal: Tension $\vec{\mathbf{T}}$ (right)[cite: 11].
  - Acceleration: $\vec{\mathbf{a}}_1$ to the right[cite: 11].

- **Block $m_2$ (suspended)**:
  - Vertical: Tension $\vec{\mathbf{T}}$ (up), Weight $m_2\vec{\mathbf{g}}$ (down)[cite: 11].
  - Acceleration: $\vec{\mathbf{a}}_2$ downward[cite: 11].

#### Significance
If the string is light and inextensible, both blocks share the same tension magnitude $T$ and acceleration magnitude $|\vec{\mathbf{a}}_1| = |\vec{\mathbf{a}}_2| = a$[cite: 11].
