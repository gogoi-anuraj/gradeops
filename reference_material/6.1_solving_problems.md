## Problem-Solving Strategies

We follow here the basics of problem solving presented earlier in this text, but we emphasize specific strategies that are useful in applying Newton’s laws of motion. Once you identify the physical principles involved in the problem and determine that they include Newton’s laws of motion, you can apply these steps to find a solution.

### Problem-Solving Strategy: Applying Newton’s Laws of Motion
1. **Identify the physical principles** involved by listing the givens and the quantities to be calculated.
2. **Sketch the situation**, using arrows to represent all forces.
3. **Determine the system of interest**. The result is a free-body diagram that is essential to solving the problem.
4. **Apply Newton’s second law** to solve the problem. If necessary, apply appropriate kinematic equations from the chapter on motion along a straight line.
5. **Check the solution** to see whether it is reasonable.

---

### Grand Piano Example Breakdown

When lifting a grand piano:
- **Identify System of Interest**: The piano itself is circled as the system of interest.
- **External Forces**: Tension $\vec{\mathbf{T}}$ pulling up and Weight $\vec{\mathbf{w}}$ pulling down.
- **Exclusion of Internal Forces**: The downward force $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{T}}$ exerted *by* the piano on the rope is an external action on the rope/world and is **omitted** from the piano's free-body diagram.
- **Equilibrium Condition**: If the piano is stationary, $\vec{\mathbf{T}} + \vec{\mathbf{w}} = 0 \implies \vec{\mathbf{T}} = -\vec{\mathbf{w}}$.

When resolving two-dimensional forces, Newton's second law is written in component forms:
$$\sum F_x = m a_x, \quad \sum F_y = m a_y$$

### Example 6.1: Different Tensions at Different Angles

Consider a traffic light ($m = 15.0\text{ kg}$) suspended from two wires. Wire 1 makes an angle of $30^\circ$ with the horizontal, and Wire 2 makes an angle of $45^\circ$ with the horizontal. Find the tension in each wire.

![Figure 6.3](figure_6_3_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.3 A traffic light suspended from two wires and its corresponding free-body diagrams.*

#### Strategy
Choose a Cartesian coordinate system with $x$ horizontal and $y$ vertical. Apply Newton's second law for static equilibrium ($\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{net}} = 0$).

#### Solution

**1. Horizontal Axis ($x$-axis):**
$$\sum F_x = T_{2x} - T_{1x} = 0 \implies T_1 \cos 30^\circ = T_2 \cos 45^\circ$$
$$T_2 = \frac{\cos 30^\circ}{\cos 45^\circ} T_1 = \frac{0.8660}{0.7071} T_1 = 1.225 T_1 \tag{Equation 1}$$

**2. Vertical Axis ($y$-axis):**
$$\sum F_y = T_{1y} + T_{2y} - w = 0 \implies T_1 \sin 30^\circ + T_2 \sin 45^\circ = mg$$

**3. Substitute Equation 1 into Vertical Equation:**
$$T_1 (0.500) + (1.225 T_1)(0.7071) = (15.0\text{ kg})(9.80\text{ m/s}^2)$$
$$1.366 T_1 = 147\text{ N} \implies T_1 = 108\text{ N}$$

**4. Solve for $T_2$:**
$$T_2 = 1.225 (108\text{ N}) = 132\text{ N}$$

#### Significance
$T_2$ is greater than $T_1$ because Wire 2 is oriented more vertically than Wire 1.

### Example 6.2: Drag Force on a Barge

Two tugboats push a barge ($m = 5.0 \times 10^6\text{ kg}$). $F_1 = 2.7 \times 10^5\text{ N}$ in the $+x$ direction, and $F_2 = 3.6 \times 10^5\text{ N}$ in the $+y$ direction. The observed acceleration is $a = 7.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m/s}^2$ at an angle of $53.1^\circ$ relative to the $+x$ axis. What is the drag force $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{D}}$ of the water?

![Figure 6.4](figure_6_4_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.4 Top view of tugboats pushing a barge and the corresponding free-body diagram.*

#### Solution

**1. Magnitude and Direction of Applied Force ($\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{app}}$):**
$$F_{\text{app}} = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2} = \sqrt{(2.7 \times 10^5)^2 + (3.6 \times 10^5)^2} = 4.5 \times 10^5\text{ N}$$
$$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{F_2}{F_1}
ight) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3.6 \times 10^5}{2.7 \times 10^5}
ight) = 53.1^\circ$$

**2. One-Dimensional Equation Along Motion Line:**
$$F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{app}} - F_{\text{D}} = ma$$
$$F_{\text{D}} = F_{\text{app}} - ma$$
$$F_{\text{D}} = (4.5 \times 10^5\text{ N}) - (5.0 \times 10^6\text{ kg})(7.5 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m/s}^2) = 7.5 \times 10^4\text{ N}$$

The direction of $\vec{\mathbf{F}}_{\text{D}}$ is directly opposite to motion ($53.1^\circ$ South of West).

### Example 6.3: What Does the Bathroom Scale Read in an Elevator?

A $75.0\text{-kg}$ man stands on a scale in an elevator. Calculate the scale reading $F_{\text{s}}$:
- **(a)** Elevator accelerates upward at $a = 1.20\text{ m/s}^2$.
- **(b)** Elevator moves upward at constant speed ($v = 1.0\text{ m/s}$).

![Figure 6.5](figure_6_5_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.5 Forces acting on a person on a scale in an elevator.*

#### Solution

**General Equation:**
$$F_{\text{net},y} = F_{\text{s}} - w = ma \implies F_{\text{s}} = m(g + a)$$

**(a) Upward Acceleration ($a = +1.20\text{ m/s}^2$):**
$$F_{\text{s}} = (75.0\text{ kg})(9.80\text{ m/s}^2 + 1.20\text{ m/s}^2) = (75.0)(11.0) = 825\text{ N}$$

**(b) Constant Velocity ($a = 0$):**
$$F_{\text{s}} = m(g + 0) = (75.0\text{ kg})(9.80\text{ m/s}^2) = 735\text{ N}$$

> **Check Your Understanding 6.1**  
> Calculate the scale reading when accelerating downward at $a = 1.20\text{ m/s}^2$.  
> - **Solution**:  
>   $$F_{\text{s}} = m(g - a) = (75.0\text{ kg})(9.80 - 1.20) = (75.0)(8.60) = 645\text{ N}$$

### Example 6.4: Two Attached Blocks

Block 1 ($m_1$) on a frictionless horizontal table is connected by a string over a frictionless pulley to Block 2 ($m_2$) hanging vertically. Find $a$ and $T$.

![Figure 6.6](figure_6_6_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.6 Two connected blocks and their respective free-body diagrams.*

#### Solution

**Block 1 (Horizontal):**
$$\sum F_x = T = m_1 a$$

**Block 2 (Vertical, downward acceleration $a$):**
$$\sum F_y = T - m_2 g = -m_2 a$$

**Solving System of Equations:**
$$m_1 a - m_2 g = -m_2 a \implies (m_1 + m_2)a = m_2 g$$
$$a = \left(\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}
ight)g$$
$$T = \left(\frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}
ight)g$$

> **Check Your Understanding 6.2**  
> Calculate $a$ and $T$ when $m_1 = 5.00\text{ kg}$ and $m_2 = 3.00\text{ kg}$.  
> - **Solution**:  
>   $$a = \left(\frac{3.00}{5.00 + 3.00}
ight)(9.80\text{ m/s}^2) = \frac{3}{8}(9.80) = 3.68\text{ m/s}^2$$  
>   $$T = (5.00\text{ kg})(3.675\text{ m/s}^2) = 18.4\text{ N}$$

### Example 6.5: Atwood Machine

Two masses $m_1 = 2.00\text{ kg}$ and $m_2 = 4.00\text{ kg}$ are connected by a string over a frictionless pulley. Find acceleration $a$ and tension $T$.

![Figure 6.7](figure_6_7_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.7 Atwood machine diagram.*

#### Solution

**System Equations:**
1. $T - m_1 g = m_1 a$
2. $T - m_2 g = -m_2 a$

Subtracting Equation 2 from Equation 1:
$$(m_2 - m_1)g = (m_1 + m_2)a \implies a = \left(\frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}
ight)g$$
$$a = \left(\frac{4.00 - 2.00}{4.00 + 2.00}
ight)(9.80\text{ m/s}^2) = \frac{2}{6}(9.80) = 3.27\text{ m/s}^2$$

**Tension $T$:**
$$T = m_1(g + a) = (2.00\text{ kg})(9.80 + 3.27) = 26.1\text{ N}$$

> **Check Your Understanding 6.3**  
> General formula for tension $T$ in an Atwood Machine:  
> - **Solution**:  
>   $$T = \frac{2 m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} g$$

## Newton’s Laws of Motion and Kinematics

### Example 6.6: What Force Must a Soccer Player Exert to Reach Top Speed?

A $70.0\text{-kg}$ soccer player accelerates from rest to $8.00\text{ m/s}$ in $2.50\text{ s}$.
- **(a) Average Acceleration:**
  $$a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{8.00\text{ m/s}}{2.50\text{ s}} = 3.20\text{ m/s}^2$$
- **(b) Average Forward Force:**
  $$F_{\text{net}} = m a = (70.0\text{ kg})(3.20\text{ m/s}^2) = 224\text{ N}$$

> **Check Your Understanding 6.4**  
> If the player stops and then experiences a force of $126\text{ N}$ to cover $x = 2.00\text{ m}$, how long does it take?  
> - **Solution**:  
>   $$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{126\text{ N}}{70.0\text{ kg}} = 1.80\text{ m/s}^2$$  
>   $$x = \frac{1}{2}a t^2 \implies t = \sqrt{\frac{2x}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(2.00)}{1.80}} = 1.49\text{ s}$$

### Example 6.7: What Force Acts on a Model Helicopter?

$m = 1.50\text{ kg}$, $\vec{\mathbf{v}}_0 = 5.00\hat{\mathbf{j}}\text{ m/s}$ at $t=0$, $\vec{\mathbf{v}}_f = (6.00\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 12.00\hat{\mathbf{j}})\text{ m/s}$ at $t = 2.00\text{ s}$.

#### Solution
$$\vec{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\Delta \vec{\mathbf{v}}}{\Delta t} = \frac{(6.00\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 12.00\hat{\mathbf{j}}) - 5.00\hat{\mathbf{j}}}{2.00} = 3.00\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3.50\hat{\mathbf{j}}\text{ m/s}^2$$
$$\sum \vec{\mathbf{F}} = m\vec{\mathbf{a}} = 1.50(3.00\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 3.50\hat{\mathbf{j}}) = 4.50\hat{\mathbf{i}} + 5.25\hat{\mathbf{j}}\text{ N}$$
$$F = \sqrt{(4.50)^2 + (5.25)^2} = 6.91\text{ N}$$

> **Check Your Understanding 6.5**  
> Find the direction of the resultant force on the model helicopter.  
> - **Solution**:  
>   $$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{5.25}{4.50}
ight) = 49.4^\circ \text{ above $+x$ axis}$$

### Example 6.8: Baggage Tractor (Time-Dependent Force)

Tractor ($m_{\text{tr}} = 650.0\text{ kg}$), Cart A ($m_{\text{A}} = 250.0\text{ kg}$), Cart B ($m_{\text{B}} = 150.0\text{ kg}$). Driving force $F(t) = 820.0 t\text{ N}$.

![Figure 6.8](figure_6_8_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.8 Baggage tractor pulling carts.*

#### Solution

**(a) Speed after $t = 3.00\text{ s}$:**
$$m_{\text{sys}} = 650.0 + 250.0 + 150.0 = 1050.0\text{ kg}$$
$$a(t) = \frac{F(t)}{m_{\text{sys}}} = \frac{820.0 t}{1050.0} = 0.7810 t\text{ m/s}^2$$
$$v(t) = \int_{0}^{3.00} a(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{3.00} 0.7810 t \, dt = \left[ 0.3905 t^2 
ight]_0^{3.00} = 0.3905 (9.00) = 3.51\text{ m/s}$$

**(b) Tension $T$ in Cable to Cart A at $t = 3.00\text{ s}$:**  
Consider the tractor alone:
$$\sum F_x = 820.0 t - T = m_{\text{tr}} a(t)$$
$$(820.0)(3.00) - T = (650.0)(0.7810 \times 3.00)$$
$$2460 - T = 1523 \implies T = 937\text{ N} \approx 938\text{ N}$$

### Calculus Relation for Acceleration vs. Position

Using $dt = \frac{ds}{v}$ and $dt = \frac{dv}{a}$, we get the fundamental calculus differential identity:
$$a \, ds = v \, dv \quad \text{or} \quad a \, dy = v \, dv$$

### Example 6.9: Motion of a Projectile Fired Vertically

A $10.0\text{-kg}$ mortar shell is fired vertically upward at $v_0 = 50.0\text{ m/s}$. Air resistance is $F_{\text{D}} = 0.0100 v^2\text{ N}$. Find maximum height $h$.

![Figure 6.9](figure_6_9_placeholder.png)  
*Figure 6.9 Free-body diagram for vertical projectile with drag force.*

#### Solution
$$\sum F_y = -F_{\text{D}} - w = m a_y$$
$$-0.0100 v^2 - (10.0)(9.80) = 10.0 a_y \implies a_y = -0.00100 v^2 - 9.80$$

Using $a_y dy = v dv$:
$$(-0.00100 v^2 - 9.80) dy = v dv \implies dy = -\frac{v \, dv}{0.00100 v^2 + 9.80}$$

Integrating from $y = 0$ ($v = 50.0\text{ m/s}$) to $y = h$ ($v = 0$):
$$\int_{0}^{h} dy = -\int_{50.0}^{0} \frac{v \, dv}{0.00100 v^2 + 9.80} = \int_{0}^{50.0} \frac{v \, dv}{0.00100 v^2 + 9.80}$$
$$h = \left[ 500 \ln(0.00100 v^2 + 9.80) 
ight]_0^{50.0} = 500 \left( \ln(12.3) - \ln(9.80) 
ight) = 500 \ln(1.255) = 114\text{ m}$$

> **Check Your Understanding 6.6**  
> If air resistance is neglected ($F_{\text{D}} = 0$), find maximum height $h$. Is calculus required?  
> - **Solution**:  
>   $$v^2 = v_0^2 - 2gh \implies 0 = (50.0)^2 - 2(9.80)h \implies h = \frac{2500}{19.6} = 128\text{ m}$$  
>   No, calculus is not required when acceleration is constant ($a = -g$).
